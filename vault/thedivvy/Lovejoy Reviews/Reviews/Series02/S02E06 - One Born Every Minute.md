# Post Episode Thoughts

## The angle

Mat's provisional read, before watching in full: **a 4, because the sexual
politics have not aged well.**

That is the spine of the review rather than a caveat to bolt on at the end.
The episode is fond of Ashley Wilkes. He is charming, he is played by a very
good actor, he gets the best lines, and the programme lets him leave with his
dignity and a shrug. What he actually does is identify women who are lonely
or neglected, paint their house for two hours, take around £200 off them, and
sleep with them. The HRT speech makes the target selection explicit and is
played as a bit of roguish theory rather than as what it is.

The women are mostly written as marks. Deborah is angry with herself rather
than with him — "I was old enough to know better." Roz is the one who gets an
inner life, and even she is passed between two men who both want her back.
Alison Jukes, who runs a business and refuses to help Lovejoy, comes off best
precisely because she is not one of the conquests.

Worth holding all of it at once: Wilkinson is excellent, the episode moves
well, and the thing it finds charming is a man defrauding women. The review
does not need to be cross about it — noticing it plainly is enough.

## The name is the joke

Ashley Wilkes is the character Leslie Howard plays in *Gone With The Wind*.
The episode holds this back until the last act and gives it to Tinker, who
gets there by admitting he always falls asleep at the same point in the film —
when Scarlett returns to Tara. "Even his name's a lie!"

Also running throughout: "An ill-favoured thing, but mine own", which Wilkes
uses as a chat-up line at every cottage. It is Touchstone in *As You Like It*.
Tinker uses it himself in the closing scene, which is the pay-off.

## The Batang Kali material — handle carefully

Buried in the last ten minutes is something genuinely unexpected for BBC1 on a
Sunday. Roz's father was a brigadier who removed documents from the Ministry
of Defence. The documents concern a patrol from his regiment in Malaya during
the Emergency, at a village called Batang Kali: 25 Chinese rubber plantation
workers taken prisoner, and shot the next day, with the official line being
that they were trying to escape. Another officer present is now "a prominent
citizen". Two men from an unnamed agency lean on Lovejoy with the threat of
Brixton.

**This is real.** On 11 and 12 December 1948, a Scots Guards patrol killed 24
unarmed villagers at Batang Kali in Selangor during the Malayan Emergency. The
British government's account was that they were insurgents shot while trying
to escape — which is, almost word for word, the cover story the episode puts
in Roz's mouth. Six of the soldiers confessed to the Metropolitan Police in
1970; the investigation was stopped for reasons those involved described as
political. Relatives lost their final UK legal battle in 2015.

Two notes for drafting:
- The episode says 25; the figure on the record is 24. Worth stating plainly.
- This is an atrocity and a cover-up that survivors' families were still
  litigating twenty-four years after this episode aired. It should not be
  written up as a fun bit of trivia. The interesting, sayable thing is that a
  Sunday-night antiques comedy reached for it at all, in 1991, and got the
  official lie right.

## Plot beats

- Lovejoy is broke, and the episode is explicit: behind on rates, three months'
  rent owed, cheques bouncing, four dealers unpaid, last quarter's garage bill
  outstanding. Chatfield reads the list aloud as leverage. £200 a day, three
  days up front.
- Miriam needs an engine and gearbox — £1,000. She gets one in the last scene,
  paid for by Wilkes without quite realising: "Someone just bought me one."
  "Who?" "You."
- The con: Wilkes turns up at a pretty cottage, paints it in two hours, sells
  it to the woman of the house for around £200, and sleeps with her. Lovejoy
  works out the pattern by collecting the paintings rather than the women.
- Chatfield's cover story is a mural in the officers' mess. He actually wants
  Roz back. Wilkes took her.
- Tinker cycles to Braintree and enrols in a weekend art school to find him.
  "Go down a hick, come back a Hockney." Rates: landscapes two to three, still
  lives three to four, nudes four.
- Alec Statham gets the muck-spreader gag — how to age brass handles, the
  answer being ammonia in cow urine, delivered to a customer's face.
- **The HRT speech.** Wilkes explains his success as hormone replacement
  therapy: a generation of mature, experienced, well-off women with, as he has
  it, nowhere to put their energy while their husbands chase temps. Quite a
  speech for 1991, and the episode neither endorses nor punishes him for it.
- Chatfield on the class system: officers have ladies, NCOs have wives, other
  ranks have women. Delivered by Wilkes, mocking him.
- The Deborah scene where Lovejoy bluffs about knowing her husband Harry and a
  trick with a poker, then gets caught out by phone later — they don't own a
  poker and she has never met anyone called Harry through him.

## Guest notes

- **Tom Wilkinson (1948-2023)** — RADA, West End debut as Horatio in 1980.
  This is six years before most people would have known his face. He was
  nearly fifty when *The Full Monty* (1997) made him internationally known and
  won him a BAFTA. Two Oscar nominations: Best Actor for *In the Bedroom*
  (2001) and Best Supporting Actor for *Michael Clayton* (2007). Golden Globe
  and Emmy for Benjamin Franklin in *John Adams* (2008). Died 30 December 2023.
- Mat's note: this episode is one of the ones he thinks of when he thinks of
  Lovejoy, along with *The Judas Pair*, *The Napoleonic Commode* and the
  Joanna Lumley run — all pre-dating this review project.
