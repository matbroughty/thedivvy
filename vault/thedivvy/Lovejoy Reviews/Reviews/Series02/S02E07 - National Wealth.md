## The review

It starts abroad, which usually doesn't augur well.

No subtitles, either. We are simply dropped into France, where a château is being stripped and two gendarmes discover that architectural salvage can be a surprisingly physical business. One of them gets a length of timber across the back of the head before the titles have finished.

So this is another one in the mould of *Death in Venice*. Lovejoy as Bond. Foreign villains, foreign police, boats, and an antique dealer from East Anglia gradually being promoted into an action hero.

Historically this has not gone well.

*National Wealth* isn't as bad as *Death in Venice*. Parts of it are very good. But it isn't classic *Lovejoy* either, and Janey is absent.

Back home, Eric has returned from the Welsh fishing trip that kept him out of *One Born Every Minute*. Nothing more is said about it. I don't think Eric ever expresses the slightest interest in fishing again, so this is the *Lovejoy* equivalent of Uncle Bryn's trip: something happened in Wales and it is probably best for everyone that we never speak of it.

The three of them are reading about a château in northern France sold to a Japanese corporation for ten million francs, its value substantially reduced by thieves who got there before the auction. Eric wants to know whether Lovejoy Associates is ready for 1992 — the episode went out in February 1991, so the single market was still eighteen months off and every business page in the country was asking the same question. Lovejoy's response is that his number one executive decision is whose turn it is to make the tea.

Then he turns to camera and explains architectural theft.

After an entire episode of no wall-breaking — *One Born Every Minute* gave us a knowing wink and nothing else — it is good to have him talking to us again. And it isn't a throwaway. This isn't somebody slipping a Constable under his coat. These people are taking fireplaces, statues, panelling and anything else they can physically wrench out of a building.

> "They tear the sinews and the heart out of the nation. They strip it of its national wealth."

Hence the title. And then:

> "What have we got left in England, if not our past?"

A grand speech from a man whose big managerial contribution ninety seconds earlier was working out who was on tea. But that's *Lovejoy*.

Then the phone rings, and Tinker takes a call for one of Lovejoy Associates' field agents. Madelene Gilbert, retired film star, wants Lovejoy to come to Carlton Grange at three.

At the mention of her name Tinker leaves the room to change, and comes back looking like Toad of Toad Hall.

Madelene Gilbert, it turns out, was one of the great screen idols of Tinker's youth. He reels off *Waterloo Bound*, *Midnight in Mayfair*, *The Lady in Black* and the apparently unforgettable *Goddess of Sahara* before announcing that by jingo, he is one of those who would give their right arm to meet her, and instructing Lovejoy to drive on.

There is a nice bit of business here where Lovejoy says the films are a bit before *our* time, Tink — quietly filing himself and Tinker in the same generation. Ian McShane and Dudley Sutton were nine years apart. The programme has always played Tinker as a man from an earlier world, and it gets away with it entirely on Sutton's delivery.

When they finally meet her, he goes full fanboy: she stole his heart when he was very, very young and he has kept it ever since.

> "How sad."

Wonderful.

Madelene needs to sell part of her collection because of a death-duty problem, and the death-duty problem is a chimpanzee. Her late husband made over a deed of gift to Jo-Jo — entered on the paperwork as John Joseph — and omitted to mention to the authorities that John Joseph was a chimp. The authorities have now noticed and want the original duty, substantial penalties and interest.

This is perfectly normal *Lovejoy* territory.

Her butler Michael doesn't entirely trust the reputation of the man they have invited in, so Lovejoy is given a divvy test: two pieces of silver, one repro and one genuine. He barely needs to look. The plated one is early 19th century, leaden pitch infill, moderate quality, about £8,000. The other is a Paul de Lamerie from around 1730 — a real name, and about as good a one as English silver has, which is why the fifty grand and up doesn't sound like showing off.

Then Madelene turns it round on him.

> "You passed my test as soon as you entered the room. One can tell everything about a man by his deportment. If one knows how to look."

The divvy has been divvied. It is a lovely scene, and she gets a Milton quotation in as well — time will run back and fetch the age of gold — which Lovejoy ducks by claiming he thought he was here to talk about antiques.

And there is a good small detail underneath it. Eric never met her at all. He walked up the drive, couldn't get past Michael, and left a business card. That was enough. Eric Catchpole, field agent, has won the firm a proper job without Lady Jane arranging it for them, which after two series of Janey opening every door is genuine progress.

When Eric starts thinking about the commission, Lovejoy explains that antiques aren't just any old commodity — they have a relationship with their owners. How would Eric feel, he asks, if he found a stranger picking his way through his Led Zeppelin collection?

Eric gets it immediately. There are more important things than money.

Remember that.

Tinker, meanwhile, celebrates by returning to the pub, settling in at the bar and observing that life is nothing but an endless process of decay.

Then: my usual, thank you.

Peak Tinker.

The opening quarter is great. Lots of Tinker, lots of Eric, and Lovejoy actually dealing in antiques. All three of them are in it together and the programme doesn't need anything more complicated than an old film star reluctantly selling her things.

Then the collection needs an auctioneer, and we get Delauneys and Mel Ford.

Lovejoy walks in asking for Mel Ford, assumes the young woman in front of him is the secretary, and is briskly informed that Melanie Ford *is* the auctioneer. He is apologetic for several seconds and then asks her to lunch. She points out that if she had been a man, as he expected, he would not be asking. Certainly not, he says. At least he doesn't pretend.

Mel is young, blonde, Cambridge-educated, half-French and ambitious, and she seems inexplicably interested in Lovejoy. McShane was 48 when this was made; Edita Brychta was 29. Maybe the 90s were different.

And this is roughly where the episode starts to sag.

Miriam breaks down, so Mel gives him a lift — to the enormous house where her father is house-sitting while the owner is abroad. Then she fancies a swim. Then we get a swimming costume scene and one thing leads to another with extraordinary efficiency.

The resulting seduction sequence is more cringe than Tinker and his army mate in *One Born Every Minute*, which is not a sentence I expected to write two weeks running.

No Janey, a French opening and a weird seduction scene means this episode is dropping points quicker than Spurs.

The morning after is better, largely because Lovejoy rings the pub sounding like a man who has recently found religion — the green grass, the birds on the wing, the whole wondrous nature of planet Earth becoming a perfect mystic fusion. Tinker diagnoses it in one line about Lovejoy getting his oats.

Unfortunately, while Lovejoy has been appreciating planet Earth, somebody has had a very good go at nicking Madelene Gilbert's house. Paintings and furniture gone, panelling gone, some of the floorboards gone. And the staircase.

Michel Le Mor — the Frenchman we watched flatten a gendarme in the opening scene — turns out to be one of Mel's previous squeezes, and he looks about the same age as Lovejoy, which rather undercuts the fuss the episode makes about Mel's youth.

Lovejoy at least has a comprehensive alibi. Mel supplies it to Detective Sergeant Armstrong in the plainest available terms: they spent most of the time in bed, making love.

An alibi Lovejoy needed rather more than I did.

Madelene has no insurance — her husband didn't think they needed it and said they couldn't afford the premiums — so there is no cushion at all. And then Mel dumps him, which leaves Lovejoy without the woman or the antiques. Tinker's summary is that there are two things in life Lovejoy holds dear, and Eric supplies them: women and antiques. Right now both have been taken away from him.

It is Eric, though, who asks the question that saves the episode. Nicking a painting is one thing, but a staircase? How do you even move something like that, never mind sell it?

Where do you sell a staircase?

And suddenly there is a proper antiques mystery again.

Lovejoy works the architectural-salvage yards — Tinker reckons there are only three or four in the whole of London dealing in that quality — and is pointed at a Major Sullivan, ex-SAS, hard as a rock, operating out of a boat moored near Windsor. Lovejoy introduces himself as Roger Hyatt of Hyatt and Hyatt Antiques, Epsom. Sullivan asks for a card.

> "Left them in the Porsche."

He is not convincing.

The police are already watching Sullivan, and Armstrong hauls Lovejoy into a car to explain that the man he has just been bothering is a Falklands veteran who served in Northern Ireland and advises Scotland Yard and Interpol. Lovejoy claims he was asking about buying medals and is threatened with the river. We also meet Adjudant Alain Bernard of the French gendarmerie, who has been chasing this gang for years and wants to talk about a murdered policeman.

At which point Lovejoy has to explain the plot to us while driving.

Never a good sign. Last witnessed in the second half of the *Death in Venice* debacle.

It does at least buy us a view of Windsor Castle, where one of my favourite kings is buried — Edward IV, who started building St George's Chapel in the first place and then ended up in it. A nice Wars of the Roses crossover in the middle of a programme about fireplaces.

What follows may be the reason the episode survives.

Lovejoy sends Tinker and Eric to watch the *Ocean Roamer II* in the middle of the night, and Tinker's first move is to suggest obtaining further refreshment on the way. Eric sits about listening to rock music on headphones. The engines start. Sullivan leaves. Neither of them notices.

They have lost a boat.

Not a bloke slipping out of a back door. Not a Sierra disappearing into traffic. A boat.

So they decide to give chase, and they hire a punt.

> "When I said hire a boat, I meant hire a boat with an engine!"
>
> "This is all they had left."

Completely ridiculous, completely them, and a nice callback to *The Firefly Cage*, the last time Tinker found himself on the water. Booze was involved that time too — he gave up the last of his whisky to soak a mop so Lovejoy could set fire to it. The man has form.

Then Lovejoy gets there. The French château was on the water. Carlton Grange was on the water. Sullivan has a boat.

That's how you steal a staircase. You float it away.

It is a good solve, and it is the only real piece of divvying in the back half. What never quite gets explained is why Sullivan has become the main man in all this — he arrives as a tip-off from a salvage yard and is running the operation by the third act with no particular reason given.

The next target is Higham Hall, which looks a bit like the White House sans the Trump extension, and by now the gang's operation has become enormous. Trucks, boats, a helicopter. Michel asks Sullivan how he intends to move a garden statue and gets told he'll make her fly. Of course he will. The budget has plainly gone up this week — although I still don't see how the *Ocean Roamer II* fits an entire house in it, staircase included, even with a tug alongside.

The final act is utterly bonkers. The crooks are in the process of taking everything, and then the gendarmes and the Met both turn up at once, in an act of Anglo-French collaboration that would be handy in Normandy at the moment.

And somewhere in the middle of an international armed-police operation, Lovejoy is taking an inventory. Two 18th-century chiffoniers. Four Bergama rugs. Four Hepplewhite dining chairs, Eric.

That is why *National Wealth* gets away with it. *Death in Venice* gradually forgot what programme it was. This one has Lovejoy standing in the middle of what has become a Bond film, still looking at the furniture.

Michel gets away on his boat, pursued by the French policeman, Lovejoy and Mel — but Tinker has got himself aboard and nobbles the engine, so Michel switches to a dinghy and we get a Bond-like chase which ends with him in the water shouting for Lovejoy, of all people, to help him. Then Bernard shoots him.

Which is the point to mention that the man is called Le Mor. One letter off *le mort*, and he is the only person in the episode who ends up dead. This is the same programme that gave Ded Lizards to *Montezuma's Revenge*, so it is fair to assume the joke is deliberate.

Tinker saves the day. Sort of.

A truly ridiculous episode that is actually quite entertaining.

And for once, the antiques actually look like they might be worth something.

Madelene's collection is convincing: good furniture, serious silver and a house full of things that look as though they have been accumulated rather than borrowed from the props department that morning. The de Lamerie test works partly because you can believe either piece might matter. That continues when the thieves start stripping the houses — panelling, fireplaces, rugs, floorboards and an entire staircase. *National Wealth* is unusually good at making the antiques look like national wealth.

Which makes it slightly unfortunate that by the end somebody is dangling them from a helicopter.

Back at Carlton Grange, Madelene wants to say thank you. Lovejoy gets the silver from his divvy test, and asks whether she is sure it isn't the original de Lamerie. Of course not, she says. It's a copy.

Whether we believe her is left entirely to us, which is the right call.

Tinker, after an entire episode in awe of one of the great stars of his youth, is offered whatever he likes and asks for her autograph. Perfect.

Then Mel turns up at Lovejoy's needing £8,000 for her father's bail — he was working with Michel — and Lovejoy looks at the silver he has just been given. Which, if you were paying attention in the first twenty minutes, he valued at about eight grand.

There are more important things than money, he told Eric.

> "Easy come, easy go. We'll get him out. In the morning."

And then another love scene.

## Favourite Moment

Tinker and Eric losing a boat.

Not a man. Not a car. A boat, with engines, on a river, in the dark, while both of them were sitting there looking at it. And then the decision to pursue it — by punt, because it was all they had left.

Everything around it is helicopters and Interpol. That bit is two blokes and a pole.

> "By jingo! I'm one of them. Drive on!"

## Guest Focus — Jean Kent & Ronald Fraser

Madelene Gilbert is Jean Kent, and the casting is the best joke in the episode because it isn't a joke.

Kent was a genuine article: signed to Gainsborough Pictures during the war, and one of the four Gainsborough Girls alongside Margaret Lockwood, Phyllis Calvert and Patricia Roc. *Fanny by Gaslight* in 1944 was the turn, and *Caravan*, *Good Time Girl* and *Trottie True* followed. She was 69 when this was filmed and had been off screens for long enough that the retirement in the script needs no acting at all.

Which means Tinker's reverence isn't invented. *Waterloo Bound* and *Goddess of Sahara* are made up, but the woman being fawned over really was one of the biggest stars in British cinema, and the script knows it — Tinker's explanation of why the butler is trustworthy is that he was her dresser at Gainsborough Pictures. That is the actual studio. Somebody in the writing room did their homework.

She died in Bury St Edmunds in 2013, aged 92, which puts her in Suffolk at the end. Fitting for this programme.

And the butler is a returning face, which is the bigger news. Michael Edwards is Ronald Fraser, who was in the very first episode of *Lovejoy* — *The Firefly Cage*, five years earlier, credited as Drummer. Same show, different man, nobody mentions it.

Fraser was one of those faces that turn up everywhere in British film without ever quite headlining: Sergeant Watson in the original *The Flight of the Phoenix*, *The Killing of Sister George*, *Too Late the Hero*, *The Wild Geese*, and the sitcom *The Misfit*. Nearly fifty films, mostly comedies, usually as somebody you wouldn't lend money to. He died in 1997, six years after this.

He is very good here, and doing something quite specific — the butler is the one who doesn't trust Lovejoy, sets the divvy test, breaks the news of the robbery and stops his employer walking into the wreckage of her own drawing room. Eric's theory that the butler did it is the natural conclusion and the episode is careful to close it off: forty years of service, and if she goes he inherits the lot anyway.

## The Divvy Verdict

A 3.5. *National Wealth* should be worse than it is.

Lovejoy-as-Bond is rarely good *Lovejoy*, and for a while this looks like going the same way as *Death in Venice*: a foreign opening, an international gang, a policeman from another country and the plot being explained to us through a windscreen. Then a helicopter arrives.

But there is a difference, and it is a simple one. The antiques never completely disappear, and neither do Tinker and Eric. The first quarter is proper *Lovejoy* — Eric accidentally winning the firm some work with a business card, Tinker dressed as Toad of Toad Hall, a film star divvying the divvy, death duties caused by a chimpanzee, and the gang arguing about the emotional relationship between people and their possessions. Even once the plot goes berserk, Tinker and Eric are there to puncture it by losing an enormous boat and setting off after it in a punt.

What holds it at 3.5 is everything in between. Janey is missed — this is the second episode running without her, and the second running where somebody else has to do her job of getting Lovejoy through a front door. The Mel romance does nothing for me and the swimming-pool business is the episode's low point. And whenever Lovejoy starts solving international crime rather than trying to make a few quid out of an antique, the series mislays some of what makes it special.

Underneath it, though, this is an episode about people losing things they cannot replace, and it is more serious about that than the helicopters suggest. A woman is selling her possessions because of a paperwork error involving a chimp, and then has them taken anyway, and she has no insurance. The thieves aren't after value, they are after the parts of a building that cannot be put back. Lovejoy's speech to camera is about a nation; the plot is about one elderly woman's staircase. Both work, because they are the same point at different sizes.

And then he hands over the silver for a bail bond, having spent the episode telling Eric there are more important things than money. Easy come, easy go. Underneath the gendarmes, the SAS major, the swimming costumes and Tinker chasing an international criminal in a punt, that is quite a neat little ending.

Unlike *Death in Venice*, it is never boring.

# Post Episode Thoughts

## Research — verified

- **Aired Sunday 17 February 1991.** One week after *One Born Every Minute*, so the weekly cadence holds through Series Two.
- **Written by Geoff Lowe. Directed by William Brayne.** Lowe is new to the site. Brayne also directed *Montezuma's Revenge* (S02E04), so this is his second.
- **Jean Kent**, 29 June 1921 – 30 November 2013. Signed to Gainsborough Pictures during the war and one of the four Gainsborough Girls with Margaret Lockwood, Phyllis Calvert and Patricia Roc. *Fanny by Gaslight* (1944) was the breakthrough; *Caravan* (1946), *Good Time Girl* (1948), *Trottie True* (1949). Aged 69 at filming. Died at Bury St Edmunds, aged 92. The four films Tinker names are invented.
- **Ronald Fraser**, 11 April 1930 (Ashton-under-Lyne) – 13 March 1997. **He is a returning face:** credited as Drummer in *The Firefly Cage* (S01E01), five years earlier, and already named in that review's `guestStar` field. *The Flight of the Phoenix* (1965) as Sgt Watson, *The Killing of Sister George* (1968), *Too Late the Hero* (1970), *The Wild Geese* (1978), and the sitcom *The Misfit*. Around fifty films.
- **Mat's age check on McShane and Sutton is right.** McShane b. 29 September 1942, Sutton b. 6 April 1933 — nine years, not a generation. The line that prompts it is Lovejoy's, not Tinker's: "Bit before our time, Tink."
- **Edita Brychta** b. 6 September 1961, so 29 at filming against McShane's 48. Mat's "approaching 50" and "around 30" both stand.
- **Paul de Lamerie** (1688–1751) is real, and is the top of the tree for English Huguenot silver. A 1730 piece at £50,000-plus in 1991 money is not a script exaggeration.
- **Madelene's quotation is Milton** — "Time will run back and fetch the age of gold", from the Ode on the Morning of Christ's Nativity. Lovejoy is asked whether he knows his Milton and ducks it.
- **The 1992 talk is topical, not anachronistic.** The single European market began on 1 January 1993 and was universally called "1992" in the British press at the time this aired.
- **Edward IV** began St George's Chapel, Windsor, in 1475 and is buried there. Mat's Wars of the Roses point is sound.
- **Eric's absence from S02E06** was written out in dialogue — "He's in Wales." "Fishing." His return here is unremarked.
- **The Firefly Cage callback is accurate.** That episode ends with a boat chase in which Tinker gives up his remaining whisky to douse a mop so Lovejoy can set fire to it.
- **The £8,000 symmetry is in the script** and looks deliberate. The plated piece is valued at "about £8,000" in the divvy test; Mel needs "about 8,000" for the bail; Lovejoy hands over the silver. Nobody in the episode points it out, and neither does the review beyond one clause — it is better left as a spot for the reader.

## Corrections to Mat's notes

Flagged rather than quietly written around, per the house rule.

- **"Michelle"** is Michel Le Mor throughout.
- **"Mel Forde who runs Delauneys"** — she is Delauneys' auctioneer at the Cambridge sale room, not the owner. Her ambition is London head office and then the international circuit. The dead man is Sam Barns, Tinker's original suggestion: "Old Sam Barns is dead Sam Barns."
- **"Muriel"** is Miriam. Silently corrected, as always.
- **Sullivan's service record.** The salvage-yard tip calls him ex-SAS; it is Armstrong who supplies the Falklands and Northern Ireland detail plus the Scotland Yard and Interpol advisory work. Both kept, attributed correctly.
- ~~**Michel being shot.**~~ **Settled by Mat on a re-watch: he is shot in the water by Bernard, the French gendarme.** The supplied transcript carries no gunshot, which is a gap in the transcription rather than in the episode. Written as the shooting.
- **Le Mor / le mort.** Mat's catch. The one character who dies is named one letter off the French for the dead man. Same writing room that named a band Ded Lizards in S02E04, so the review treats it as deliberate — but nothing confirms intent, so it is phrased as "fair to assume", not as fact.
- **"Lj rumbles that"** — the note breaks off mid-sentence. Taken as the water insight (French château on water, Carlton Grange on water, Sullivan has a boat), which is what the scene actually is.
